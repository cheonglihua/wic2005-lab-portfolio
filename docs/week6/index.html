D6

What I learned

Design, isolation, flood and learn, MTU fixes and the P4 alternative.

design

Describe your design: hypervisors, tenants, VNIs and addresses. Which tenants overlap, and why did you place each VM where you did?

My data centre is four racks, rack1 to rack4, on the underlay 10.3.0.0/24 at 10.3.0.1 to 10.3.0.4. The underlay runs jumbo frames (MTU 9000), my chosen MTU fix. It hosts three tenants, with VNIs taken from my ID: shop (VNI 4901, 10.3.50.0/24), bank (VNI 4902, also 10.3.50.0/24) and lab (VNI 4903, 10.3.70.0/24). shop and bank overlap on purpose. They are two customers who both picked the same private range, which is normal in a shared cloud, so only the VNI can tell their VMs apart.

shop's web tier is spread over three racks: web1 (.10) on rack1, web2 (.11) on rack2 and web3 (.12) on rack3. The shop survives losing any one rack, and every shop VTEP has two other VTEPs in its flood list. bank's two payment VMs are on different racks for anti-affinity: pay1 (.10) on rack1 and pay2 (.11) on rack3. I put them on the same racks as web1 and web3 to make isolation as hard as possible. rack1 and rack3 each host two tenants, and pay1/web1 and pay2/web2 have the same IP addresses. lab runs dev1 on rack2 and dev2 on rack4. That makes rack2 host two tenants with different networks, and makes rack4 a hypervisor outside shop's VNI, so I could check that shop's floods do not reach it.

isolation

Two of your VMs have the same IP address. Using your evidence, explain how a ping reaches the right one — and what would have to go wrong for one tenant to see another's traffic.

web2 (shop, rack2) and pay2 (bank, rack3) are both 10.3.50.11. When web1 pinged 10.3.50.11, all 3 replies came back, and web1's ARP entry is lladdr 02:00:13:25:00:0b. The middle bytes 00:13:25 are 0x1325 = 4901, so it is shop, and 0b is .11, so it is web2. A tcpdump on pay2 at the same time captured 0 packets. This is true even though rack3 did receive shop's flooded ARP request (it is in flood.txt). rack3 decapsulated that request into vxlan4901, which is in br4901 with only web3's port, and pay2's port rack3-eth2 is in br4902. When pay1 pinged the same address, it got lladdr 02:00:13:26:00:0b (0x1326 = 4902, bank), so pay2 answered. Same IP, two different MACs: the answer depends on which bridge, and so which VNI, the sender's port is in. The overlay never looks at the IP address.

For one tenant to see another's traffic, the VNI mapping would have to break. Examples: a VM's port put in the wrong bridge (such as rack3-eth2 master br4901 because of an off-by-one in the port numbers), two tenants given the same VNI (check_spec() refuses that), or a VXLAN device created with the wrong id. The other risk is the underlay. VXLAN has no authentication or encryption, so anyone who can send UDP to port 4789 on a rack could inject frames with VNI 4902 and reach bank. The isolation is only as good as the trust in the underlay (IPsec or MACsec would close that).

flood learn

Using your evidence, explain what was flooded, to which hypervisors, and what each VTEP learned. With 50 hypervisors in one VNI, how many copies would one broadcast need — and how would EVPN change that?

Before any traffic, rack1's vxlan4901 table held only my two flood entries: 00:00:00:00:00:00 dst 10.3.0.2 and dst 10.3.0.3, both permanent (from tenant_commands()). web1 pinged web2 at 10.3.50.11. Its first frame, an ARP broadcast, went into br4901 and out of vxlan4901, which made one copy per flood entry. rack3's underlay capture shows that copy, 10.3.0.1 > 10.3.0.3.4789, vni 4901, ARP who-has 10.3.50.11 tell 10.3.50.10, even though the conversation was between rack1 and rack2. It shows only that one packet: the ARP reply and the ICMP echoes were unicast, because by then the MACs were learned. rack4 captured 0 packets because it is not in shop's flood list.

Afterwards each VTEP had learned MAC-to-VTEP entries from the outer source IP of what it received. rack1 learned 02:00:13:25:00:0b (web2) dst 10.3.0.2. rack2 learned 02:00:13:25:00:0a (web1) dst 10.3.0.1. rack3 also learned web1 at 10.3.0.1 from the flood alone, although it never answered. The learned entries are not permanent, so they age out.

With 50 hypervisors in one VNI, head-end replication needs 49 copies of every broadcast or unknown-unicast frame, all sent by the source hypervisor. Each hypervisor's flood list also has 49 entries to maintain by hand. EVPN puts a BGP control plane in front of this. Type 3 routes build the flood lists automatically. Type 2 routes advertise each VM's MAC and IP when it is attached, so remote VTEPs know where every MAC is before any traffic, with no data-plane learning. With ARP suppression, the local VTEP answers ARP from that table, so most broadcasts never leave the hypervisor. EVPN still needs 49 copies for the broadcasts that remain, unless the underlay does multicast, but those broadcasts become rare.

mtu

Compare your two MTU fixes using your measurements. Which would you choose for a data centre, what does each cost, and who has to change what?

check_mtu() refused blackhole.yaml for every tenant: 1500 + 50 = 1550, 50 bytes more than the 1500-byte underlay. Built anyway with --force, small pings worked, but 1472-byte pings lost 100% and iperf3 moved 0 bytes in 5 seconds, with cwnd stuck at 1.41 KB. The largest don't-fragment ping that worked was 1422 bytes (a 1450-byte packet). 1423 was lost silently: the VM believes its MTU is 1500, and br4901 drops the frame at vxlan4901 (MTU 1450) without telling anyone.

Fix A (tenants 1450, underlay 1500): 1422 works, and 1423 now fails at the sender with "message too long, mtu=1450", so TCP picks a smaller MSS. iperf3 got 3.08 Gbit/s. Fix B (tenants 1500, underlay 9000): a full 1472-byte ping (1500-byte packet) works, and 1473 fails locally with mtu=1500. iperf3 got 2.85 Gbit/s. In my software-only Mininet, that difference is run-to-run noise (fix A's run had 926 retransmits, fix B's 224). On real hardware, B carries 50 more bytes of payload per packet.

I would choose fix B for a data centre, and my overlay.yaml uses it. Tenants keep the standard 1500, so no VM image, appliance or customer has to change, and there is headroom for an IPv6 underlay (70 bytes) or extra headers. The cost falls on the operator. Every switch port, hypervisor NIC and router in the underlay must be set to jumbo frames, and one missed device brings back exactly the lab Part 6 black hole, so it needs monitoring. Fix A needs no network change, but every tenant VM must be configured to 1450 (by DHCP option 26 or cloud-init), loses 50 bytes of every packet, and a VM that forgets falls into the black hole. Fix A is right only when you do not own the underlay, for example an overlay across the internet.

p4

If your hypervisors' VTEPs were P4 switches running the lecture's vtep.p4, what would have to fill the vtep_encap table, and how? What would replace flood-and-learn?

In vtep.p4 both tables start empty and default to drop, so the control plane must fill them, as in the Week 5 lab (with P4Runtime or simple_switch_CLI table_add). vtep_encap is keyed on (ingress port, destination MAC). The ingress port identifies the tenant, because each VM has its own port. The encap action is given everything the packet will need: the VNI, the switch's own MAC and VTEP address, the remote VTEP, the next-hop MAC on the underlay, and the output port.

For my rack1, port 1 is web1 (shop) and port 2 is pay1 (bank). Its entries would be: (port 1, 02:00:13:25:00:0b) -> encap(4901, ..., 10.3.0.1 -> 10.3.0.2) for web2; (port 1, 02:00:13:25:00:0c) -> encap(4901, ..., -> 10.3.0.3) for web3; and (port 2, 02:00:13:26:00:0b) -> encap(4902, ..., -> 10.3.0.3) for pay2.

The matching vtep_decap entries, keyed on (VNI, inner destination MAC), would be (4901, 02:00:13:25:00:0a) -> port 1 and (4902, 02:00:13:26:00:0a) -> port 2. pay1 and web1 share 10.3.50.10, but they never collide: the keys use the port and the MAC (which carries the VNI), never the IP address.

My overlay.yaml already holds everything the controller needs. plan() gives every VM's MAC, VNI and hypervisor, and vm_ports() gives each VM's port, so a controller could generate these entries in the same way that all_commands() generates my ip and bridge commands. The underlay next-hop MACs would come from the controller's knowledge of the underlay.

Flood-and-learn would be replaced by this control plane. vtep.p4 cannot learn: a P4 program cannot write its own tables, and it has no ARP and no flooding, so a frame for an unknown MAC is dropped, not flooded. Every MAC must be installed in advance, from the orchestrator's inventory or from EVPN type 2 routes translated into table entries. ARP must also be handled another way, for example by the controller answering it or by static neighbour entries in the VMs. The cost is a controller that must be told about every VM before it can talk. The gain is no flooding at all, and no 49-copy broadcasts.
