What I learned

Describe your design: why these routers, LANs and links, and how you chose the addresses.

My design uses three routers in a triangle, with h1 on r1 and h2 and h3 on separate LANs behind r2. The direct r1â€“r2 link gives h1 a short path to h2, while the path through r3 provides a second physical route between the routers. I used the lab's address plan with NN=03: LANs use 10.3.1.0/24, 10.3.32.0/24, and 10.3.33.0/24, and each router link has its own /30.

Which route summarises several LANs? Which networks does it cover, why is it correct, and what else would it attract if you added a network inside its range?

The 10.3.32.0/20 route summarizes the 10.3.32.0/24 and 10.3.33.0/24 LANs because both are inside 10.3.32.0 through 10.3.47.255. It lets r1 send traffic for the remote LAN block through r3, while its more specific 10.3.32.0/24 route sends h2 traffic directly toward r2. The summary would also attract any future network inside that /20, including unused ranges such as 10.3.40.0/24, unless a more specific route or another design change handled it.

When you took a link down, what happened, and why couldn't your routes recover even though another path existed? What would have recovered?

When I took r1â€“r2 down, h1's ping to h2 lost all packets. The route lookup on r1 then selected the broader 10.3.32.0/20 route through r3, so an alternate forward path existed. But r2 reported "Network is unreachable" for h1: its route back used the failed r1â€“r2 link and disappeared. Bringing the link up did not restore that static route, and the ping still failed. Re-adding r2's route manually restored the ping. A routing protocol such as OSPF could detect the failure and recalculate the routes automatically.

In your network, what was the control plane? Who worked out the routes, and what does that mean for a network of 40 routers?

In this lab, I worked out the routes and wrote them as static entries in network.yaml. The Linux kernel's routing table then makes the forwarding decision for each packet using longest-prefix match; there is no routing protocol calculating routes for this topology. For 40 routers, manually maintaining every route would be error-prone, so a routing protocol or a validated configuration generator would be more practical and could respond to topology changes.
